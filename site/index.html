Coxeter-MFV

Metabolic maps and flux analysis for cells and microbial communities

Coxeter-MFV is a free web app for metabolic modeling. Open a model of one organism or of a microbial community, see it drawn as cells and compartments, and run flux balance analysis on it. The results appear on the map, showing which reactions carry flux, in which compartment and in which organism. Use it to explore a model, test media and knockouts, follow cross-feeding between species and make figures.

What you can do

Open your own model

SBML, COBRA JSON, COBRA Toolbox MAT or Excel. Several models make a community. Formats and sample files.

Draw cells and compartments

Small models are laid out for you. Move, reshape and add reactions, metabolites, compartments and cells.

Analyze

FBA, pFBA, FVA, pFVA, loopless FBA, CycleFreeFlux, SteadyCom and GIMME, with any objective.

Change conditions

Edit the medium, bounds, reaction and gene knockouts and species abundances, then solve again.

Study communities

See which metabolites pass between species, with the Cross-Feeding Explorer and lines on the map.

Add omics data

Overlay gene expression, proteomics or metatranscriptomics, and build context-specific models with GIMME.

Export

Maps as SVG or PNG, fluxes as CSV or JSON, models as SBML or COBRA JSON, and a methods report.

Reproduce

Save a bundle of model, map, settings and solution, with checksums, that solves again when opened.

Offline and desktop

Download the app as a single HTML file (18 MB) and open it with no internet connection. A Windows desktop version, with the HiGHS solver and optionally Gurobi or CPLEX, is in the repository.

FAQ

  1. What is Coxeter-MFV?

    A web app that draws metabolic models as maps of cells and compartments and runs flux balance analysis on them. To start, choose an example above and press Open the app, or read the help.

  2. Do I need to install anything or create an account?

    No. It runs in a current web browser with no login. To work offline, download the single-file app.

  3. Are my models uploaded?

    No. Every calculation runs in your browser, and models, maps and results stay on your computer. The app keeps your current work in this browser's storage so that it survives a reload. There is no cookie and no tracking. The Yeast9 and phenylalanine rescue examples download public model files, and database links open only when you click them. The site is hosted on GitHub Pages.

  4. Which files can I open?

    Models in SBML Level 3 with FBC, COBRA JSON, COBRA Toolbox MAT or Excel. Maps, scenarios and reproducibility bundles saved by Coxeter-MFV. Fluxes computed elsewhere, as Escher reaction data or CSV. Gene expression, proteomics and metatranscriptomics tables as CSV or TSV.

  5. Can I open Escher maps?

    Not as maps: Coxeter-MFV draws its own maps, with cells and compartments. It reads and writes Escher's reaction data, so fluxes move between the two tools.

  6. How large can a model be?

    Models of up to about 350 reactions per organism are laid out automatically. Larger ones start from an overview of core metabolism that you can extend. Genome-scale models such as Yeast9, with 4105 reactions, solve in the browser.

  7. Which solver does it use?

    GLPK, compiled to WebAssembly, in the browser. The Windows desktop version uses HiGHS, or Gurobi or CPLEX when they are installed.

  8. What browsers can I use?

    A current version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari. We test in Chromium and Firefox. Editing maps works best with a mouse or touchpad.

  9. How do I cite Coxeter-MFV?

    Cite the software and the version you used, as given in CITATION.cff. The article reference will be added here on publication.

  10. How is Coxeter-MFV licensed?

    The source code is under the MIT License. The app includes glpk.js and is distributed under GPL-3.0. Bundled models keep their original licenses (model terms).

  11. I have more questions. Who do I ask?

    Start with the help. To report a bug or ask a question, open an issue on GitHub, with your browser and a small example.

About the name

Coxeter-MFV stands for Coxeter Multicell Flux Visualizer. The name honors the geometer Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter (1907-2003), whose work on nested geometric structures and symmetry groups inspired the app's hierarchical representation of cellular compartments: mitochondria inside the cytoplasm, inside cells, inside a shared medium. His figures of hyperbolic tilings also inspired M. C. Escher's Circle Limit prints.

Coxeter-MFV is developed at the Center for Mathematical Modeling, University of Chile.
